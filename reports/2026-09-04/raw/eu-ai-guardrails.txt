CHAPEL HILL, N.C. —
The Trump administration used this week's G20 innovation summit to draw a bright line between U.S. and
European
approaches to
AI
regulation. The bloc's top tech official says the U.S. is ending up with many of the same guardrails anyway.
Why it matters:
The U.S. is urging countries to avoid Europe's sweeping approach to AI regulation, but the fight is shifting to which guardrails advanced AI needs and how governments put them in place.
What they're saying:
European Commission tech chief
Henna Virkkunen
told Axios that the two sides often end up addressing the same safety concerns, even if U.S. safeguards are starting to emerge through state laws and court cases rather than something broad like the EU AI Act.
"We see when it comes to technologies that in the USA the culture is different. So often it's coming via court cases, but often very similar things anyway, very similar concerns."
"I don't see that this is so like black and white," Virkkunen said.
ChatGPT is officially
facing
strict rules under the Digital Services Act, for instance, and Virkkunen said it's important for the EU to simplify the bloc's rules and make the region "easier, simpler, faster" for businesses.
The big picture:
The U.S. hasn't adopted sweeping federal AI rules, but Washington is still
shaping
how companies develop and deploy advanced models through executive actions, voluntary frameworks, national security restrictions and other policy.
Meanwhile, states are moving ahead with varying degrees of AI regulation, and
product changes
are being forced on companies through lawsuits.
Zoom in:
The EU is working on a framework for pre-deployment testing and trusted partner access, Virkkunen noted, another area where the EU is working through similar AI policy questions facing the U.S.
"So in [the]
European Union, we are also working for that blueprint, and we are continuing to discuss also under G7, especially with our partners, how we should work on this topic."
The Trump administration has
developed
a voluntary framework for reviewing some advanced AI models before release, including potentially giving government officials access for up to 30 days. The White House does not plan to
publicly
release this framework.
OpenAI's Sam Altman
confirmed
this week to Axios that the administration reviewed its Astra model and noted the process is voluntary: "But we of course did it."
Industry leaders at the G20 innovation summit
knocked the EU approach to regulation in remarks before Virkkunen as well as representatives from Germany, France and Italy.
In response to White House Office of Science and Technology Policy Director Michael Kratsios' question about what foreign governments should and should not do, SpaceX CEO Elon Musk said an approach that's "relatively free of regulation" is the way to go.
It's "pretty straightforward," Musk said. "You have to have an environment that's relatively free of regulation, meaning that new things must be default legal as opposed to default illegal."
"In the EU, for example, we find that the regulation level is extraordinarily high, and things are generally default illegal, and this inhibits progress with new technologies. It slows it down."
The bottom line:
As AI has grown more powerful, Washington has increasingly gotten involved with model releases.
