The search for a winning message on
AI
is pushing candidates and lawmakers into unexpected political territory.
Why it matters:
With the midterms approaching, AI is creating
alliances
across party lines while opening fissures within them.
Here are three ways
AI is scrambling the political map.
1. Centrist Democrats versus progressives on data centers
New York made waves
as the
first state to impose
a data center moratorium — a product of Gov. Kathy Hochul joining forces with progressive state Sen. Kristen Gonzalez. But the two policymakers are just getting started on reconciling their differing
approaches
to AI.
Gonzalez previously advocated for a three-year moratorium and is working to extend the one-year pause in Hochul's executive order. She's spearheading a statewide tour to advocate for Hochul to sign her Responsible Data Center Development Act.
Gonzalez is going beyond Democratic strongholds, with her tour reaching into red and purple parts of upstate New York and the Hudson Valley.
Where Hochul and Gonzalez land is an early test of just how much
progressive and centrist Democrats
can align on an issue that's mobilizing voters across the political spectrum.
What they're saying:
"We need Gov. Hochul to step up and sign the bill and provide full protection to the people of New York," Sustainable Finger Lakes president Gay Nicholson said in a call with reporters ahead of the tour.
"Please just don't leave our small rural towns with limited staff and resources at the mercy of an extractive industry that enriches themselves at the expense of upstate New Yorkers," said Nicholson, whose organization is among those advocating for Gonzalez's bill.
Zoom out:
For a brief moment, Hochul and President Trump were
sounding similar
, until Hochul took it a step further with her moratorium executive order.
"If Hochul is serious about lowering costs, she should embrace the Trump Administration's energy agenda," a White House official told Axios in a statement following the signing of Hochul's executive order. "Democrats want to stifle innovation and weaken America's competitive edge over China in the AI race."
2. Trump world versus Tea Party advocates on data centers
Activists with ties to the Tea Party movement
are protesting data centers, calling on Republicans to "get their heads out of the sand and stop pretending like this is not an issue, because it is an issue," Humans First spokesperson Chris Barron said.
Humans First
describes itself as a grassroots conservative organization fighting for an "America First AI policy," and its chair, Amy Kremer, is a longtime Tea Party figure.
The group, however, is being disavowed by
conservative outlets
and facing accusations of being an astroturf organization aligned with the left.
The intrigue:
Barron, a Trump surrogate in 2016, pushed back against criticism that liberals holding anti-Trump signs showed up at data center protests last month organized by Humans First, noting that "the foundational building blocks" of MAGA included converting people into Republican voters.
"We've been loyal supporters of the president from the very beginning," Barron said, adding, "I feel confident that at the end of the day, the president will understand what the base of his party wants."
The other side:
While Humans First says it represents the MAGA base and conservatives, sources familiar with the administration say there's no alignment with the organization.
"There is no one in this group that has any access, visibility, or influence in the White House at all," one source told Axios, referring to Humans First.
3. Libertarians and progressives on surveillance
Congressional Progressive Caucus Chair
Greg Casar (D-Texas)
has begun strategizing
for a Democratic takeover of the House, eyeing common ground with libertarian lawmakers on the right.
The two factions have historically aligned on surveillance, but AI is giving the issue a bigger playing field, and congressional leaders like Casar plan to take advantage.
Casar is focusing on privacy and closing surveillance law loopholes that allow brokers to collect data from Americans through corporations.
In places like Pennsylvania and
West Virginia
, Republicans are zeroing in on AI-powered surveillance and joining Democrats on legislation to
create safeguards
against government and industry misuse of personal data.
The bottom line:
Don't look for clean Republican-versus-Democratic platforms on AI ahead of the elections.
