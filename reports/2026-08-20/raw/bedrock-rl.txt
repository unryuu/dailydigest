Minecraft is close to an ideal testbed for embodied vision-language agents: open-ended, spatial, and full of long-horizon tasks with programmatically verifiable outcomes. Historically, however, it has been a challenging environment to optimize RL in. The Java client is slow, non-deterministic, and hostile to parallel rollouts, so most prior work settled for behavior cloning on human video.
bedrock-rl
fixes the plumbing. It couples
Netherite
, a deterministic C/CUDA reimplementation of the Minecraft simulation, with
verl
for distributed RL, and wraps both in a framework of replaceable components. You define a world and its reward once in YAML, generate reproducible seed-disjoint train/dev/test episodes, and train a VLM with GRPO, RLOO, REINFORCE++, ReMax, GSPO, SFT, SDFT, SDPO, or on-policy distillation, all against the same unchanged verifier.
Everything is a component
There is no registry, no plugin API, and no fork. Every piece of an experiment is a
package.module:object
import path selected in YAML, and each layer only depends on the layer below it (architecture tests enforce the import directions):

Synthetic data
A generation job is
task + sampler + qualifier + policy + executor + writer
. Scripted survival experts plan from each natural world's snapshot and emit the same computer actions the model will emit. The executor runs isolated C-engine episodes across every CPU, or across a Modal fleet as a headless seed scout followed by exact pixel replay on a smaller machine. The writer keeps only trajectories that passed the unchanged task verifier, publishes parquet and a rejection ledger atomically, and every row carries the world seed, decision seed, snapshot hash, and provenance needed to reproduce it.
One design point we care about: a scripted expert can see the whole voxel map, a frame-only VLM cannot. Policies declare per-turn
grounding
, and the SFT writer refuses privileged decisions (for example, a clairvoyant route to underground iron) by default. Behavior-cloning targets are only actions the model could have inferred from what it saw.
