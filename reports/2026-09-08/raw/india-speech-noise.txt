ARTPARK-IISc
A human-annotated noise layer on Project Vaani's spontaneous multilingual speech, timestamping 106,892 real-world noise events to the millisecond.
The Vaani Noise Event Timestamp Dataset: a new annotation layer that marks exactly when a dog barks, a scooter passes, or a baby cries, right down to the millisecond.
Speech AI has become remarkably good at hearing us, as long as we speak in a quiet room. Real life is not quiet, and the moment speech AI meets that reality, accuracy drops. The Vaani Noise Event Timestamp Dataset, a new annotation layer built on top of
Project Vaani
, is designed to address this limitation, with precise temporal localization of noise events as its key focus.
Every background noise event is annotated with its type and its exact start and end time, and events can overlap with speech and with each other.
The problem with "just add noise"
Most speech datasets are recorded in studios or clean conditions, so the models trained on them quietly assume the world is quiet.
The usual fix is
synthetic mixing
: overlaying a separate noise clip on top of a clean speech recording.
That makes the audio
sound
noisy, but it discards the one thing a model most needs to learn: when the noise happens, relative to the words being spoken.
Synthetic noise also behaves unnaturally. The signal-to-noise ratio is set by hand, the noise starts and stops abruptly, and any overlap with speech is engineered rather than real.
Even India-specific datasets such as Kathbath-Noisy provide noisy speech for evaluating ASR robustness, but they do not annotate what type of noise occurred, or when it occurred in the audio.
What makes this dataset different
The key contribution is structured, temporally localized annotation of noise events, capturing both their type and their timing in the audio.
Speech and noise were
recorded together
, at the same time, in the same place, on ordinary mobile phones out in the field, without synthetic noise insertion or post-hoc noise mixing.
Annotators marked every background noise event with an exact
start and end time, down to the millisecond
.
Because the recordings are real, noises
overlap with speech and with each other
, just like real conversation. A dog can bark mid-sentence while traffic rumbles underneath and a phone rings on top.
Background sounds are sorted into
seven categories
: animal noises, traffic, babies and children, music, phone and alarm signals, appliances, and non-speech human sounds such as coughs, laughs, and lip smacks.

Coverage is deliberately broad, spanning everything from Hindi and Telugu to genuinely low-resource languages like Chakma, Garo, and Mizo.
Hindi dominates the collection at nearly 84 hours, but the dataset intentionally keeps a long tail of underrepresented languages so that noise-robustness research does not skew toward only the most common ones.
Seven kinds of everyday noise
Non-speech human sounds are the most frequent noise type but the briefest; animal and traffic sounds are rarer yet account for the most total noise time.
Non-speech human sounds
(coughs, laughs, lip smacks) are the single most common noise type, appearing in about 38% of segments, but they are fleeting, averaging less than half a second each.
Animal and traffic sounds
are far less frequent, yet they last much longer, and together they account for the largest share of the dataset's total noise time.
This split, frequent-but-brief versus rare-but-sustained, is exactly the kind of structure a model needs to handle real audio well, and it only becomes visible once every event has a start and end time attached.
Built to be trusted
The dataset ships in two clearly labeled tiers: a
verified_timestamps
set (about 22 hours), confirmed by agreement between multiple annotators, and an
unverified_timestamps
set (about 100 hours), so researchers can decide exactly how much to trust each clip.
Every timestamp passes through a multi-stage, human quality-control pipeline: freelancer annotation, a sanity check, internal re-verification, and a random 10% independent audit before release.
The bar is deliberately strict. If even a single timestamp fails the audit, the
entire batch is redone
, an unusually demanding standard for a dataset this size.
Why no existing dataset fills this gap
The team benchmarked Vaani against nine major existing datasets, including WHAM!, AudioSet, MUSAN, CHiME-6, and FSD50K.
None of them combine real, co-occurring background noise, millisecond-level timestamps, and natural Indic multilingual speech in one place.
Existing options fall into two camps: Western and English-centric (CHiME-6, AVA-Speech), or Indian but lacking noise annotation entirely.
Two India-specific noise datasets already exist, iNoise and Kathbath-Noisy, but neither pairs noise with the natural, co-occurring speech that Vaani captures.
This makes Vaani a reusable benchmark for the conditions Indian voice AI actually ships into. Any system meant to work in Indian households, streets, or farms (an IVR line, a voice assistant, an agri-advisory bot) can be stress-tested against the specific noise types it will really encounter, rather than a generic Western urban noise profile.
What it unlocks
Noise-robust speech recognition
: the kind that keeps working when you are talking on a crowded street or in a busy kitchen, not just in a silent room.
Sound event detection and speech enhancement
: because the noise is timestamped and real, AI systems can learn to identify, locate, and filter out real-world background noise far more accurately than synthetic mixtures allow.
A low-resource dividend
: the noise layer rides on Project Vaani's existing multilingual coverage, extending noise-robustness research to languages like Chakma, Garo, and Mizo that have essentially zero existing noisy-speech resources, a contribution that is novel not just in India, but globally, for low-resource ASR.
The bigger goal
: voice AI that actually works in everyday Indian conditions (mobile phones, regional languages, and the noisy, unscripted backdrop of real life), instead of performing well only in sterile lab settings. Getting there starts with something small and precise: knowing exactly when the dog barked.
